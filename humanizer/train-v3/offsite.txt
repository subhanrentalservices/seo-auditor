=== K1 keyboard, plain Claude explainer
Cleaning a keyboard takes about ten minutes and needs only a few basic supplies. Unplug it first, or switch it off if it's wireless. Turn it upside down and tap it gently to shake loose crumbs and dust. Then use a can of compressed air to blow out what's left between the keys, holding the can upright and working in short bursts. For the key tops, dampen a microfiber cloth with isopropyl alcohol and wipe each key. A cotton swab helps with the edges. Avoid spraying liquid directly onto the keyboard, since it can seep under the keys and damage the circuitry. If a key feels sticky, you can usually pop the keycap off with a keycap puller and clean underneath it. Let everything dry fully before plugging the keyboard back in. Doing this once a month keeps buildup under control and makes the keyboard last longer.

=== K2 keyboard, news-blog register, hedged, aside
Most keyboards can be cleaned in around ten minutes, and the job doesn't really call for anything more than a few household supplies. It is generally a good idea to unplug the keyboard first (or switch it off, if it's a wireless one) and then turn it over and give it a few gentle taps, which tends to dislodge a surprising amount of crumbs and dust. Compressed air will usually shift whatever is left between the keys, provided the can is held upright and used in short bursts. The tops of the keys can be wiped with a microfiber cloth lightly dampened with isopropyl alcohol, with a cotton swab for the edges, although liquid should never be sprayed straight onto the keyboard, since it can work its way under the keys and into the circuitry. A key that feels sticky can often be lifted off with a keycap puller so that the area underneath can be cleaned, and the whole thing should be left to dry properly before it is plugged back in.

=== H1 history topic, plain Claude explainer
The Great Fire of London began on 2 September 1666 in a bakery on Pudding Lane. It spread quickly through the city's narrow streets, where timber houses stood close together. A dry summer and a strong east wind made it worse. The fire burned for four days and destroyed about 13,200 houses and 87 parish churches, including the old St Paul's Cathedral. The recorded death toll was low, but historians think the real number was higher, since the deaths of poor and middle-class people often went unrecorded. Afterwards, the city was rebuilt with wider streets and buildings of brick and stone. Christopher Wren designed many of the new churches, including the St Paul's Cathedral that stands today.

=== H2 history topic, news-blog register, hedged
The Great Fire of London is generally said to have started in the early hours of 2 September 1666, in a bakery on Pudding Lane, and it spread with alarming speed through streets where timber houses were packed closely together. A long dry summer and a strong east wind did not help matters. By the time it was brought under control four days later it had, by most accounts, destroyed some 13,200 houses and 87 parish churches, the old St Paul's Cathedral among them. Only a handful of deaths were ever recorded, although historians have long suspected the true figure was a good deal higher, since the deaths of poorer Londoners were often never written down. The rebuilding that followed brought wider streets and a shift to brick and stone, and Christopher Wren was given the job of designing many of the new churches, including the St Paul's that still stands today.
